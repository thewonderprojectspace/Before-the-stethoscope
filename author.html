A Letter from the Founder

A Letter to Humanity.

From one human being to another.

Wherever you are in life, whatever brought you here, you are welcome to stay for a little while. There is no hurry.

Read at your own pace. This letter can wait.

Before the Stethoscope

For the Human Within

♡

Dear fellow human,

Before anything else, I hope you know that you are welcome here.

You do not need a degree, a title, a particular belief, a certain background or a perfectly understood life to belong. You do not need to have everything figured out. In fact, I am still figuring out quite a lot myself.

I do not know what life has asked of you, what you have lost, what you have overcome, or what you are quietly carrying today. But I hope that, for these few moments, you can simply exist without having to explain yourself.

Where it all began

A year and a half ago, I began working on Before the Stethoscope with a simple intention: to make a difference in healthcare education. What started as an attempt to connect science with humanity through writing has gradually become something much more personal.

Somewhere between writing about cells, tissues, illness and the extraordinary complexity of human life, I found myself returning to a much simpler question.

How can we understand so much about the human body, yet sometimes struggle so deeply to understand one another?

Medicine asks us to learn an extraordinary amount: how cells work, how systems respond, how illness changes the body, and how evidence can guide decisions. It can be easy to let the volume of information become the whole purpose.

We spend years learning how the heart beats, how the brain communicates and how the body responds to injury. Yet understanding the mechanisms of life does not automatically teach us how to understand the experience of living.

These books begin with a person because science becomes meaningful when it helps us understand something about a life. Each chapter follows a question through the underlying mechanisms, then returns to the person and asks what the science explains—and what still needs care, curiosity or further understanding.

A person experiencing breathlessness is not simply an opportunity to discuss respiratory physiology. A person living with cancer is not merely an example of disrupted cell division. They are individuals whose lives, relationships, hopes and understanding of the future may have changed.

I never want the beauty of scientific knowledge to overshadow the significance of the person it is intended to serve.

And to those of you who are learning, please be patient with yourselves.

Learning takes time. Some ideas will make sense quickly; others may need another explanation, a new diagram or a pause before they settle. Use the questions to practise your thinking, return to the chapters when you need them, and let understanding grow through steady effort.

You are allowed to be confused.

You are allowed to ask the same question twice.

You are allowed to take longer than the person sitting beside you.

Education should never make you feel that your worth depends on how quickly you understand something.

I hope these books help you see the science clearly—and remember the person at its centre.

Thank you for reading and for bringing your own questions to the work. I hope these books become something you can return to with curiosity rather than fear, and with a genuine desire to understand rather than simply remember.

The beautiful contradiction of being human

For a long time, I have questioned the world we live in.

Why should the quality of healthcare someone receives depend on where they were born? Why should access to education, safety, nourishment and dignity be so different from one person to another? Why have we made something as fundamental as caring for one another so complicated?

We have drawn borders across the earth, and somewhere along the way, we have also drawn borders between ourselves.

We have divided ourselves through nationality, language, religion, wealth, profession, social position and countless other identities. These identities can be meaningful and beautiful, but they should never become reasons to forget our shared humanity.

And here is something I find rather funny, in the most beautiful sense of the word.

Despite everything that supposedly makes us different, we all possess this extraordinary capacity to feel.

We experience love, fear, grief, hope, loneliness, excitement and the desire to belong. A mother worrying about her child on one side of the world is not so fundamentally different from a mother worrying about her child on the other. The comfort of being understood does not belong to a particular nationality. The pain of losing someone does not recognise a border.

Our experiences may be different. The intensity of our emotions may differ, and the ways in which we express them may vary enormously.

But there is something deeply familiar in our capacity to feel.

Perhaps the reason we perceive the world so differently is that life has introduced itself to each of us in a different way.

Some of us have been taught to trust. Others have learned to protect themselves. Some have experienced kindness when they needed it most, while others have experienced disappointment from the very people or institutions they believed would protect them.

Our experiences shape our perspectives, our beliefs, our fears and sometimes even the walls we build around ourselves.

I wonder how differently we might treat one another if, before judging someone's behaviour, we became a little more curious about the experiences that shaped it.

Understanding does not mean excusing harm. Kindness does not mean abandoning boundaries. Compassion does not require us to agree with everything another person believes.

But it does invite us to recognise that there is a human being on the other side of our disagreement.

Perhaps we are not as different as the world has taught us to believe.

I do not want to simply watch anymore

There have been many occasions when I have found myself frustrated with the way society functions.

I have questioned why preventable suffering continues to exist, why people feel unheard, why healthcare can become inaccessible, and why kindness sometimes feels like an extraordinary act rather than an ordinary part of being human.

But I have also had to ask myself an uncomfortable question.

What am I doing about it?

I do not want to spend my life sitting comfortably in the shade, discussing everything that is wrong with the world, while waiting for someone else to stand in the sunlight and do something about it.

I want to stand up.

Not because I have all the answers. I certainly do not.

Not because I believe I am capable of changing the world on my own. No individual can, and perhaps that is precisely the point.

I want to stand up because I believe that we have a choice in how we participate in the world around us.

We do not have to reproduce the indifference we have experienced. We do not have to continue treating unfairness as inevitable simply because it has existed for generations. We do not have to accept that the way things have always been done is the only way they can ever be done.

We can question. We can learn. We can organise. We can cooperate. We can build alternatives and work towards better systems.

We can choose not to participate in harmful patterns in the same way again.

I believe that systems are created by people, sustained by people and, ultimately, capable of being changed by people.

Authority may be held by individuals and institutions, but the capacity for collective change belongs to communities.

It belongs to the teacher who refuses to give up on a struggling student. To the nurse who takes an extra moment to listen. To the researcher who continues asking difficult questions. To the neighbour who notices when someone needs help. To the ordinary person who chooses to act with kindness when it would have been easier to look away.

These people may never appear in history books. Their names may never be celebrated. Yet their actions quietly hold our society together.

The power to build a more humane world lives within its people.

And I want Before the Stethoscope to be one small place where we begin to rediscover that power together.

A community, not a hierarchy

Before the Stethoscope is not intended to become a community in which everyone thinks the same way.

Quite the opposite.

I want to create a space where a medical student can learn from a patient, where a professor can learn from a school student, where a healthcare professional can speak honestly about their challenges, and where someone without any formal education in medicine can contribute something profoundly meaningful.

Knowledge does not exist exclusively within universities, textbooks or professional institutions.

There is knowledge in lived experience. There is knowledge in caring for someone who is unwell. There is knowledge in navigating systems that were not designed with your circumstances in mind. There is knowledge in listening, observing, questioning and simply living.

Through educational books, community gatherings, conferences, discussions and collaborative projects, I hope we can bring these different forms of knowledge together.

I want us to move beyond identifying problems and begin exploring solutions, however small those solutions may initially seem.

I want us to create an environment where people can ask difficult questions without humiliation, disagree without cruelty and share their experiences without feeling pressured to reveal more than they are comfortable sharing.

I want this to be a community where listening is valued just as much as speaking.

Where a person does not need to earn the right to be heard.

Where kindness is not confused with weakness.

And where no individual is considered more worthy of dignity simply because of the position they occupy.

You do not have to agree with everything I believe to belong here. I hope you will challenge my ideas, offer different perspectives and help me recognise things I may not yet understand.

I am learning too.

For the human within

This philosophy extends beyond patients and healthcare professionals. It extends to every one of us.

Behind every diagnosis is a person. Behind every profession is a person. Behind every mistake, achievement, disagreement, fear and ambition is a person.

Sometimes, we become so occupied with the roles we play that we forget who we were before the world began placing expectations upon us.

Before we became students, teachers, doctors, nurses, researchers, leaders or workers, we were human beings.

And that is what we will always remain.

I cannot promise that Before the Stethoscope will solve every problem. I cannot promise that we will always agree, that our efforts will always succeed, or that the journey will be easy.

But I can commit to approaching this work with care. To remaining open to learning. To acknowledging mistakes. To listening when someone tells me that something could be done better.

And, most importantly, to remembering why this began.

The individual comes before the system.

I want to live in a world where knowledge is shared rather than guarded, where healthcare is guided by human dignity, where communities support one another, and where a person's circumstances do not determine the value of their life.

Perhaps we cannot build that world overnight.

But surely, we can begin.

A conversation at a time.
A classroom at a time.
A community at a time.
A human being at a time.

An invitation, without expectations

If you have found your way here because you are curious about science, welcome.

If you are here because you have experienced something that made you question healthcare or society, welcome.

If you are here because you want to contribute, learn, teach, research, share an idea or help build something meaningful, welcome.

And if you are simply here because you needed to read something kind today, you are equally welcome.

You do not owe this community your productivity, your personal story or your participation.

Your worth is not determined by how useful you are to other people.

You are allowed to listen. You are allowed to observe. You are allowed to take your time.

I hope that Before the Stethoscope becomes a place where we can remind one another of something that should never have needed reminding.

That beneath our different experiences, identities, beliefs and circumstances, we belong to the same humanity.

We do not have to think alike to care for one another.

We do not have to live the same lives to recognise each other's suffering.

And we certainly do not need a stethoscope to care.

Thank you for being here. Thank you for taking the time to read these words. And thank you for every little act of kindness you have brought into the world, especially those that nobody was there to notice.

I hope our paths cross someday.

Until then, please take care of yourself, and whenever you can, take care of one another.

With love, hope and care,

Isha Tiwari

Founder & Author
