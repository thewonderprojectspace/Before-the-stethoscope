02 · Ask the physiology question

What must happen before one cell becomes two?

A cell needs to grow, copy its DNA once, check that the copy is ready, and distribute one complete set of chromosomes to each daughter cell. Select a phase to follow the sequence.

G1 · Grow and assess

The cell grows, performs its usual work and responds to signals. At the G1 checkpoint, it can assess conditions and DNA integrity before committing to DNA synthesis.

Relative DNA amount2C · before replication
Keep the order straight: DNA replication occurs in S phase. The chromosomes then separate during mitosis in M phase. Mitosis is nuclear division; cytokinesis divides the cytoplasm. Together they produce two cells. [1–3]

G0 describes a state outside the active cycle. Some cells can re-enter the cycle; others are highly specialised and rarely divide.

Interphase · prepareBefore mitosis, DNA has already been replicated during S phase. Each chromosome now consists of two sister chromatids joined at a centromere.
01

Prophase

Chromatin condenses into visible chromosomes. The mitotic spindle begins to form as the cell prepares to move chromosomes.

02

Metaphase

Chromosomes line up at the cell’s equator. The spindle-assembly checkpoint monitors whether kinetochores are properly attached to spindle microtubules.

03

Anaphase → telophase → cytokinesis

Sister chromatids separate and move to opposite poles. New nuclei form; the cytoplasm then divides. The result is two daughter cells with the same chromosome number as the parent cell.

Some textbooks include prometaphase as a separate stage; others group its events with prophase. The chromosome movements are the same.

03 · Trace the mechanism

Checkpoints are pauses with a purpose.

Cell-cycle checkpoints monitor whether key conditions are met. They can delay progression while problems are repaired; when damage cannot be repaired, a cell may enter apoptosis or other fates.

The major internal checkpoints are commonly described at G1, G2/M and metaphase (the spindle-assembly checkpoint). S-phase surveillance also helps detect problems during DNA replication. These controls lower risk; they cannot guarantee that every error is caught. [7–9]

Repair is not the only outcome. A cell may pause, repair damage, enter a non-dividing state, undergo senescence or die. The response depends on the type and extent of damage and on the cell.
04 · Connect the systems

Cells use both accelerators and brakes.

Growth signals and cell-cycle regulators work together. Cancer can arise when changes accumulate in pathways controlling growth, DNA repair, checkpoints or cell death. The details vary between cancers and between people.

In a simplified model, cyclin D–CDK4/6 activity can phosphorylate Rb, changing its restraint on E2F transcription factors and helping the cell progress toward S phase. This pathway is one part of a much larger network. [8, 10]

Where p53 and p21 fit

When DNA damage is detected, p53 can activate genes such as CDKN1A, which encodes p21. p21 can inhibit cyclin–CDK activity, helping pause the cycle while the cell responds to damage. Rb also helps restrain progression from G1 toward S phase.

These proteins are often called tumour suppressors because their normal functions help prevent inappropriate cell proliferation. Alterations in their pathways can remove important restraints, but cancer usually involves a combination of biological changes rather than one simple broken switch. [8, 9]

For Sana: “Cell-cycle control” helps explain one part of cancer biology. It does not describe her whole diagnosis, predict her outlook or determine her treatment. Her care team interprets her individual pathology and discusses options with her.
A careful clinical connection: Some CDK4/6 inhibitors are used with other medicines for specific breast-cancer subtypes, including certain hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative disease. That is a treatment connection to the pathway—not a treatment recommendation for Sana or for every person with breast cancer. [10]
04 · Connect the systems

Two kinds of division. Different purposes.

Mitosis supports growth, repair and tissue maintenance. Meiosis makes haploid gametes and contributes to genetic variation. They share some vocabulary, but their outcomes differ.

FeatureMitosisMeiosis
Where / purposeSomatic cells; growth, replacement and repairGerm-line cells; production of gametes
DNA replicationOnce before division, in S phaseOnce before meiosis I; no new S phase between meiosis I and II
Number of divisionsOne nuclear divisionTwo successive divisions
What separates first?Sister chromatidsHomologous chromosomes in meiosis I; sister chromatids in meiosis II
Typical outcomeTwo daughter cells with the same chromosome number as the parentFour haploid products in the standard teaching model; gamete development differs by sex and species
Genetic variationDaughter cells are usually genetically very similar, barring mutation or division errorCrossing over and independent assortment produce new combinations
Open the meiosis sequence

Meiosis I · reduce

Homologous chromosome pairs come together. Crossing over can exchange DNA between non-sister chromatids. Homologues then separate, reducing the chromosome set from diploid to haploid.

Meiosis II · separate

Sister chromatids separate in a second division. There is no second round of DNA replication between meiosis I and meiosis II.

Remember: mitosis separates sister chromatids in one division; meiosis I separates homologues, then meiosis II separates sister chromatids. [4–6]

Carry these ideas forward

Cell division is not a single switch. It is a sequence of growth, DNA replication, monitoring, chromosome separation and cell division. Control systems help coordinate each step.

For a person facing a diagnosis, physiology is one tool for understanding—not a substitute for listening, informed discussion or individual care.
